A layered country

Algeria's civilisations & culture

Amazigh, Roman, Arab, Andalusian, Ottoman and Saharan worlds layered over three thousand years — and each region still carries its own palette, craft and table.

Amazigh (Berber)

Algeria's indigenous foundation. For over three millennia the Imazighen — the “free people” — have held the mountains and oases, keeping a living language, Tamazight, now official beside Arabic. Their identity runs strongest in Kabylie, the Aurès and the M'Zab: poetry, silver, the Yennayer new year and the yaz symbol ⵣ.

Explore →

Roman

Rome held the fertile north for six centuries and left some of the best-preserved Roman cities on Earth — Djémila, Timgad and Tipaza — with arches, forums, theatres and brilliant mosaics, often standing almost alone in the hills.

Explore →

Arab & Islamic

From the 7th century, Arab dynasties brought Islam, the Arabic language and a long age of learning and building — mosques, madrasas and walled medinas. In the M’Zab, the Ibadi community raised five fortified desert towns that still live by their medieval plan.

Explore →

Andalusian

Muslims and Jews expelled from Spain settled in Tlemcen, Algiers and Constantine, bringing Andalusian music — gharnati and malouf — refined architecture and the zellij tilework and craft that still define the western cities.

Explore →

Ottoman

For three centuries Algiers was a powerful Ottoman regency. The legacy survives most vividly in the UNESCO-listed Casbah — a steep maze of Ottoman houses, palaces and mosques climbing above the bay.

Explore →

Saharan & Tuareg

The deep south is its own world. The Tuareg — the “blue people” of the veil — keep camel routes, silver crosses and a desert hospitality, against the rock-art galleries of Tassili n’Ajjer that record a green Sahara 10,000 years gone.

Explore →

Algerian cuisine

Couscous on Fridays, slow tagines, fresh-grilled coastal fish, Saharan dates, olive oil from the Kabyle hills, and mint tea poured high — Algerian food maps the country’s geography and its layered history onto the plate.

Algeria travel FAQ

Is Algeria safe for tourists?

Yes for normal tourism in the north and on organised desert trips. Cities are generally calm and welcoming; take the usual precautions you would anywhere. The deep south and border regions are restricted and are visited only with licensed operators and permits.

Do I need a visa for Algeria?

Most nationalities need a visa arranged in advance at an Algerian consulate, usually supported by an invitation/voucher from a licensed agency. Rules vary by nationality and change over time — always confirm with your consulate.

When is the best time to visit Algeria?

Spring (March–May) and autumn (September–November) are best for the north — coast, Roman cities and mountains. The Sahara is a winter destination, best from November to February.

How much does a trip to Algeria cost?

It depends on length and style. Our day tours start around €30, multi-day private tours run from a few hundred euros, and our longest 10-day private journeys are around €1,200 per person. Each tour page lists its price.

How do you get around in Algeria?

Domestic flights cover the long distances (especially to the Sahara), comfortable trains link the northern cities, and private vehicles with a driver are the easiest way to tour. In the desert, travel is by 4x4 only.

Can I use credit cards and ATMs?

Algeria is largely a cash economy. Cards are accepted only in some city hotels and rarely elsewhere, and foreign-card ATMs are unreliable. Plan to pay cash for most things.
