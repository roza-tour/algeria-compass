A layered country

Civilisations of Algeria

Eight worlds layered over three thousand years — Amazigh, Saharan, Mozabite, Roman, the first Islamic kingdoms, Andalusian, Ottoman and modern. Choose one to explore its states and the journeys that reach it.

Civilisation 01

Amazigh

Long before Rome or Islam, the Imazighen — the 'free people' — held these mountains and oases and gave the land its oldest living language, Tamazight. Their script, silver, pottery and the Yennayer new year endure, strongest in Kabylie and the Aurès.

★ Tifinagh script and Berber pottery patterns are over 3,000 years old.

Civilisation 02

Tuareg & Sahara

The deep south belongs to the Tuareg — the blue-veiled caravan people — and to a Sahara of vast dunes and rock-art plateaus. At Tassili n’Ajjer, thousands of paintings record a green Sahara that vanished millennia ago.

★ Tassili n'Ajjer holds ~15,000 rock paintings, older than the pyramids.

Civilisation 03

Mozabite M'Zab

In a dry valley of the northern Sahara, the Ibadi Mozabites raised five fortified white towns around watchtower-minarets. Their austere, ingenious urbanism has survived a thousand years — and shaped modern architecture.

★ The M'Zab's cubist architecture inspired Le Corbusier; UNESCO since 1982.

Civilisation 04

Numidian & Roman

Before and during Rome, the Numidian kings and then the empire raised cities across the fertile north. Djémila, Timgad and Tipaza survive as some of the best-preserved Roman cities anywhere — forums, theatres and mosaics standing in open country.

★ Djémila and Timgad are among the best-preserved Roman cities on Earth.

Civilisation 05

Islamic-Berber Dynasties

From the 10th century, Berber Muslim dynasties — Hammadid, Zirid and others — founded the first kingdoms of the central Maghreb, leaving brick minarets and ruined capitals across the high country.

★ The Qal'a of Beni Hammad, first Hammadid capital, is UNESCO-listed (1980).

Civilisation 06

Andalusian

When Muslims and Jews were expelled from Spain, many settled in Tlemcen, Oran and the western cities, bringing Andalusian music, zellige tilework and refined architecture. Tlemcen, the Zianid capital, became their great refuge.

★ Tlemcen was the Zianid capital and a refuge for the exiles of Al-Andalus.

Civilisation 07

Ottoman Algiers

For three centuries Algiers was the seat of Ottoman power in the Maghreb — a corsair city-state above its bay. Its legacy is clearest in the UNESCO-listed Casbah, a steep maze of palaces, mosques and Ottoman houses.

★ For 300 years Algiers led Ottoman Barbary; its Casbah is UNESCO-listed.

Safety & welcome

Is Algeria actually safe to visit?

Yes. The north — Algiers, the coast, the Roman cities, Kabylie — is calm, and Algerians are famously hospitable to the few foreigners who come. You take the ordinary precautions of any new country. The deep south and borders are restricted, and we only travel them with permits and licensed guides.

Will I actually be welcome as a foreign tourist?

More than welcome. Algeria sees a tiny fraction of its neighbours’ visitors, so you arrive as a guest, not a number — expect curiosity, invitations to tea, and very little hassle.

Is it safe for solo and women travellers?

Generally yes, with modest dress and normal awareness; serious harassment is uncommon. Travelling privately with a local guide removes most of the friction women tell us they worry about.

Visa & entry

Do I need a visa, and is it hard?

Most nationalities need a tourist visa from an Algerian consulate before travel — and consulates require an invitation letter from a licensed Algerian agency. That is exactly the part we handle for you, free, with every booking.

Is there an e-visa or visa on arrival?

Not for ordinary tourism. The main exception is organised Sahara tourism entered via the south, where a simplified group entry can be arranged. We tell you honestly which route applies — and never promise what the rules don’t allow.

Why Algeria feels “hard” — and how we remove it

Why does planning Algeria feel so difficult?

Because information is thin, English is patchy, the desert needs permits, and you can’t just book everything online. That gap is the whole reason we exist: one licensed operator handling visa paperwork, permits, flights, guides and logistics so you don’t stitch it together yourself.

Can’t I just do it independently?

In the north, with patience, yes — and we’ll say so. The Sahara, no: protected zones legally require a licensed operator, a 4x4 and permits. We’re upfront about where you need us and where you don’t.

What if something goes wrong on the ground?

You have a named local team and guide for the whole trip, reachable on WhatsApp. Nothing is “confirmed” until availability, transport and permits are actually checked — so what we promise is real.

Money, language & daily life

Can I pay by card, or is it cash?

Algeria runs on cash. Cards work in a few city hotels and foreign-card ATMs are unreliable, so bring euros or dollars to exchange on arrival. We’ll tell you roughly how much to carry.

What languages will I get by in?

Arabic and Tamazight are everyday, French is widely spoken, English less so outside tourism. Our guides bridge the gap in all four.

What’s daily life like for a visitor?

Relaxed, social and a little improvised. SIM cards are cheap, cities have wifi, the desert is gloriously off-grid — and you should stick to bottled water.

What to see & how long

How long do I really need?

A long weekend covers Algiers and the Roman coast. A week adds the M’Zab Valley or a Sahara leg. Ten days lets you cross from the Mediterranean to the dunes. Every tour page lists exactly what’s included.

First trip — where should I go?

Most first-timers love the mix of the UNESCO Casbah of Algiers, the Roman cities (Tipaza, Djémila, Timgad) and a few nights of Sahara at Djanet or Timimoun. We tailor it to your dates and pace.

When should I come?

Spring and autumn for the north; November to February for the Sahara. We plan around the seasons and Ramadan so the trip actually works.
