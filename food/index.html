Culture · Cuisine

The Food of Algeria

Six civilisations, one table — and couscous at the centre of it.

Home › Culture › Food
Quick answer

Algerian cuisine is a Maghrebi tradition built on couscous — the national dish, eaten on Fridays — alongside hearty soups like chorba, slow tagines, fresh breads and sweet mint tea. It layers Amazigh, Arab, Ottoman, Andalusian, French and Saharan influences, and changes character from the Mediterranean coast to the deep desert.

Six influences on one table

No single people made Algerian food. It is the sum of everyone who has lived on this land — the indigenous Imazighen, the Arab dynasties, three Ottoman centuries, the exiles of Al-Andalus, the French colonial era, and the Sahara itself. You taste all of them in a single week.

Amazigh

The Berber foundation: semolina, couscous, barley breads and the rhythms of the land.

Arab

Spice and sweetness — cinnamon, saffron, dried fruit and honeyed pastries.

Ottoman

Turkish technique: bourek, stuffed vegetables (dolma) and rich pastry-craft.

Andalusian

From exiled Al-Andalus: delicate sauces, almonds and the refined cooking of the western cities.

French

The colonial echo — the baguette, café culture, flan and a love of good bread.

Saharan

The desert table: dates, dried meats, flat breads baked in sand and tea poured high.

The national dish

Couscous — the centre of everything

If Algeria has one dish, it is couscous: hand-rolled semolina steamed light and fluffy over a broth of meat and seasonal vegetables. It is eaten at Friday lunch, pressed on every guest, and served at weddings and funerals alike. Berber in origin and Maghrebi to the core, it is less a recipe than a ritual of generosity.

Look for it topped with lamb shank in winter, with chicken and chickpeas year-round, and sweetened with raisins and cinnamon (masfouf) for celebrations.

Soups, starters & small plates

Chorba & Loubia

Nationwide · the evening table

Chorba is the fragrant soup that opens the meal — frik or vermicelli, tomato, lamb and herbs — and the heart of the Ramadan iftar. Loubia, a slow white-bean stew, is its hearty everyday cousin.

Bourek

Ottoman heritage · a Ramadan staple

Crisp golden cigars of thin warqa pastry rolled around spiced minced meat, egg or cheese, then fried — the sound of Ramadan kitchens across the country.

Chakchouka & Hmiss

Coast & interior · summer peppers

Slow-cooked peppers, tomatoes and garlic, sometimes finished with egg (chakchouka); hmiss is its smoky roasted-pepper salad, scooped up with grilled bread.

Hmiss dip

A mezze of roasted peppers & tomato

The roasted, chopped pepper-and-tomato relish that sits on almost every table — cooling, smoky and made to share with fresh bread.

Breads & street food

Msemen

Maghreb-wide · breakfast & tea

A square, laminated griddle-bread, flaky and tender, eaten warm with honey and butter — and never far from a glass of mint tea.

Mhadjeb

High Plateaus & centre · street food

Thin semolina flatbread folded around a slow-cooked filling of tomato, onion and pepper, griddled to order — the classic Algerian street snack.

The big plates

Couscous

Amazigh · the national dish, eaten Fridays

Hand-rolled semolina steamed over a meat-and-vegetable broth — lamb or chicken, chickpeas, carrots, courgette and turnip. Friday lunch, weddings, mourning, welcome: couscous marks them all.

Rechta

Algiers & the centre · celebration dish

Fine hand-pulled noodles in a pale, gently spiced chicken-and-chickpea sauce scented with cinnamon — a dish of Algiers for births, holidays and Mawlid.

Festive couscous

Across Algeria · the celebration table

The grand, generous platter brought out for guests — couscous crowned with meat, vegetables and chickpeas, shared from the middle of the table.

Culture · Pastries

Algerian sweets & pastries

The other half of the Algerian table. After the savoury comes a world of honeyed, almond-rich pastries — Ottoman in technique, Andalusian in delicacy, and brought out by the trayful for Eid, weddings and Ramadan nights with glasses of mint tea or coffee.

Baklawa

Ottoman heritage · weddings & Eid

Fine layers of warqa pastry packed with walnuts or almonds, baked and drenched in honey or syrup, then cut into glossy diamonds. The centrepiece of weddings and Eid tables.

Makroud el louz

Constantine & the east · almond

The refined almond cousin of Constantine's date-stuffed makroud: little almond-paste leaves poached in syrup and rolled in sugar, melting and floral.

Qalb el louz

Ramadan tables · "heart of the almond"

A semolina-and-almond cake soaked in orange-blossom syrup until dense and moist — its name means "heart of the almond", and it is the taste of Ramadan.

Samsa

Algiers & Tlemcen · Eid

Crisp triangles or rolls of thin pastry filled with ground almonds, fried or baked and dipped in honey, then scattered with sesame or pistachio.

Griwech

Nationwide · Ramadan

Ribbons of sesame dough folded into lattice roses, deep-fried golden and bathed in honey — crunchy, glossy and impossible to eat just one.

Tcharek (kaab el ghzal)

Maghreb-wide · "gazelle horns"

Slender crescents of soft pastry around orange-blossom almond paste — kaab el ghzal, "gazelle horns", the most elegant of the holiday cookies.

Dziriette

Algiers · the Algerois petit four

Dainty fluted tartlets of almond paste, delicate and pale — a refined sweet of Algiers brought out for weddings and grand occasions.

Ghribia (montecaos)

Nationwide · Eid

A shortbread of butter and flour (or chickpea flour) that dissolves on the tongue, lightly spiced — the simplest and most beloved cookie of Eid.

Mhalbi

Algiers · rice-flour pudding

A cool rice-flour milk pudding perfumed with rose or orange-blossom water and dusted with cinnamon and crushed nuts — light, fragrant and refreshing.

The sweet finish & the ritual of tea

Mint tea

The Sahara & everywhere · hospitality

Strong green tea with fresh mint and sugar, poured from a height to crown it with foam. In the desert it comes in three glasses — “bitter as life, sweet as love, gentle as death” — and is never to be rushed.

Dates

Saharan oases · Deglet Nour

The honey-coloured Deglet Nour of the southern oases — Biskra, Tolga, the Gourara — is among the finest in the world. Dates open hospitality, close a meal and fuel every desert journey. (A dedicated photograph is being sourced.)
