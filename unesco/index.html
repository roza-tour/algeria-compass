World Heritage

UNESCO World Heritage Sites in Algeria

Seven inscribed sites across one country — Roman cities, an Ottoman citadel, a fortified desert valley and the greatest open-air gallery of prehistoric art on earth.

Home › UNESCO World Heritage Sites
Quick answer

Algeria holds seven UNESCO World Heritage Sites, more than any other country in the Maghreb: three Roman cities (Timgad, Djémila, Tipasa), the Ottoman Casbah of Algiers, the fortified M’Zab Valley, the Hammadid fortress of Beni Hammad and the vast rock-art plateau of Tassili n’Ajjer. Six are in the north and reachable on one overland trip; Tassili n’Ajjer needs an internal flight and an organised desert expedition.

Seven sites is a remarkable count for a country that receives so few visitors — more than Morocco, more than Tunisia, and the reason serious travellers keep arriving with a longer list than they expected. What makes the set unusual is its range: it covers close to two thousand years of building, and one site predates all the others by several millennia.

Six of the seven sit in the north and can be strung together on a single overland itinerary. The seventh, Tassili n’Ajjer, is a Saharan plateau the size of a small country and needs a flight south and a licensed operator — it is the one that changes how people think about Algeria.

The seven sites

  1. 1

    Kasbah of Algiers

    Algiers · Inscribed 1992

    The Ottoman citadel above the bay — a dense hillside of white lanes, stairways and hidden courtyards, with palaces, mosques and the remains of the 16th-century fortifications that made Algiers a Mediterranean power.

  2. 2

    Djémila

    Sétif · Inscribed 1982

    Roman Cuicul, built 900 metres up on a narrow mountain spur, where the standard imperial town plan had to bend to the terrain. The theatre, the Severan temple and the Arch of Caracalla survive in extraordinary condition, along with a museum of mosaics.

  3. 3

    Timgad

    Batna · Inscribed 1982

    Founded by Trajan around 100 AD as a colony for army veterans, and laid out on a perfect grid — the textbook example of Roman town planning, preserved for centuries beneath Saharan sand. Its arch and 3,500-seat theatre still stand.

  4. 4

    Tipasa

    Tipaza · Inscribed 1982

    A Phoenician trading post that became a Roman port, spread along the Mediterranean shore with a forum, theatre, baths and early-Christian basilicas. Nearby stands the Royal Mausoleum of Mauretania, the 1st-century BC tomb of Juba II and Cleopatra Selene.

  5. 5

    M’Zab Valley

    Ghardaïa · Inscribed 1982

    Five fortified towns built by the Ibadi Mozabites from the 11th century, each terraced around its mosque above a palm-filled wadi. The cubic architecture and the water-sharing system that sustains it were studied closely by Le Corbusier.

  6. 6

    Al Qal’a of Beni Hammad

    M’Sila · Inscribed 1980

    Algeria’s first inscribed site: the fortified capital of the Hammadid dynasty, founded in 1007 and abandoned within 150 years. Its ruined palaces and the tall minaret of the great mosque sit alone at 1,000 metres in the Hodna mountains.

  7. 7

    Tassili n’Ajjer

    Illizi · Djanet · Inscribed 1982

    A sandstone plateau of eroded rock forests covering some 72,000 km², holding one of the world’s largest concentrations of prehistoric art — thousands of paintings and engravings recording a Sahara of rivers, cattle and hunters before the desert came.

Seeing them on one trip

The three Roman cities and the Casbah pair naturally: Tipasa is an hour west of Algiers, Djémila and Timgad sit east in the highlands, and Beni Hammad is a detour off that same road. The M’Zab is a short flight or a long drive south. Tassili n’Ajjer belongs to a different kind of journey — an organised expedition out of Djanet, which is also the route that allows entry on arrival rather than a consulate visa.
