## Why visit Illizi
Illizi is one of the largest and emptiest states in Algeria, and almost all of it is desert. What brings travellers here is the **Tassili n'Ajjer**: a sandstone plateau the size of a small country, eroded into pillars, arches and canyons, and covered — in shelters and on cliff faces across thousands of sites — with the painted and carved record of the people who lived here when the Sahara was grassland.

## A short history
The rock art is the history. The oldest engravings date back roughly **10,000 years**, to a period when this was savannah crossed by rivers, and they show elephant, giraffe, hippopotamus and crocodile. Later phases show **cattle herding**, then **horses and chariots**, and finally **camels** — a visual record of the desert forming beneath the people who lived in it.

The plateau's later history belongs to the **Tuareg**, whose Kel Ajjer confederation gave the region its name and who remain its inhabitants and its guides. **UNESCO** inscribed the Tassili n'Ajjer in **1982** on both cultural and natural criteria, an unusual dual listing.

## What to see
The **rock art sites** are scattered across the plateau and reached on foot from mobile camps — Sefar, Tamrit and Jabbaren are among the best known. Getting to them is a multi-day trek with pack animals and a Tuareg team, and that journey is much of the experience.

The **geology** stands on its own: forests of eroded sandstone, natural arches, deep guelta pools that hold water year-round, and dunes banked against black rock. To the south lies the **Tadrart**, the red sandstone country reached from Djanet, which most itineraries combine with the plateau.

## Culture
This is **Tuareg** country. The guides, drivers and cooks on any expedition here are local, the language is Tamahaq, and the hospitality — tea made three times over a fire, bread baked in the sand — is not a performance laid on for visitors.

## Getting there and around
There is no independent way to do this. Travellers **fly to Djanet** from Algiers, then continue by 4x4 and on foot with a licensed operator holding the permits. Expeditions run **November to February**; nights are genuinely cold and there is no mobile coverage once you leave town.
