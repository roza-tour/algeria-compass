## Overview
**Tassili n'Ajjer** is a high sandstone plateau in Algeria's far south-east — a landscape of wind-carved rock 'forests', canyons and dunes that doubles as one of the most important **prehistoric art sites on Earth**. It was inscribed on the **UNESCO World Heritage List in 1982**, and is reached from the oasis town of **Djanet**.

## Why it's a UNESCO World Heritage Site
Tassili is one of the relatively few **mixed** World Heritage Sites, valued for **both** its cultural and its natural significance: an open-air archive of human art spanning millennia, set within an outstanding geological landscape. The plateau's combination of dense rock art, archaeology, endemic relict cypress trees and surreal erosion is what earned it inscription.

## The rock art: an open-air gallery
Across the plateau are an estimated **15,000 engravings and paintings**, some many thousands of years old — figures of people, herds, hunts, dances and ceremonies, painted in ochres and engraved into the rock. It is less a single monument than a **landscape-sized gallery**, with art tucked into shelters and rock faces over a huge area.

## Reading the green Sahara
The images are a record of **climate change written by the people who lived through it**. The earliest show wildlife of a wet savanna — **elephants, giraffes, hippos and crocodiles** — that could only have existed when the Sahara was green. Later scenes shift to **cattle herding**, then to **horses and chariots**, and finally to the **camel**, the animal of the dry desert that exists today.

## The chronological styles
Researchers group the art into broad, overlapping periods that track that drying:
- **Round Head period** — large, enigmatic human figures, among the oldest works.
- **Pastoral (Bovidian) period** — herds of cattle and scenes of daily life, the richest phase.
- **Horse (Caballine) period** — horses and chariots as the climate dried.
- **Camel (Cameline) period** — the camel arrives, marking the fully arid Sahara.

## The plateau landscape & geology
The setting is as remarkable as the art. Millennia of wind and water have eroded the sandstone into **'forests of rock'** — pinnacles, arches and labyrinths — interspersed with dunes and seasonal pools. Stands of the rare, ancient **Saharan cypress** cling on as living relicts of the wetter past.

## Conservation & responsible visiting
The art is **fragile and irreplaceable**, which is why the plateau is a **protected zone** entered only with licensed guides and permits. Visitors are asked never to touch, wet or damage the paintings, to camp only in designated places and to leave no trace — the same care that has preserved this record for thousands of years.
