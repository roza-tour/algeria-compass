**Couscous** is the answer, and in Algeria it is less a dish than an institution. Hand-rolled semolina, steamed in a *couscoussier* over a broth of meat and vegetables, then served in a mound with the broth ladled over it — this is the Friday meal, the wedding meal, the meal that marks a birth or a mourning, and often just dinner.

In 2020 UNESCO inscribed the knowledge, know-how and practices around couscous on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, in a joint submission by **Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia and Mauritania** — a rare piece of shared Maghreb diplomacy, and a recognition that the dish belongs to a region rather than a country.

Algeria's versions vary sharply by geography. The east favours a richer, redder broth; the west a lighter one; the Kabyle mountains make it with vegetables and pulses and sometimes without meat; the Sahara serves it with camel or with dates worked into a sweet version. The *seffa* variant is sweet, with cinnamon, sugar and raisins.

Couscous is far from the whole story. **Chorba** soup opens meals through Ramadan, **rechta** is Algiers' fine-noodle speciality, **chakhchoukha** is the torn-flatbread dish of the east, and **mhadjeb** and **msemen** are the street food that keeps the country going between meals.
