Ramadan is not a reason to avoid Algeria. For a traveller interested in the country rather than a checklist of sites, it may be the most rewarding month of the year — but you have to accept that the day runs backwards.

**During daylight** the country is quiet. Most restaurants and cafés are shut, offices and sites keep shortened hours, traffic thins in the afternoon, and people are conserving energy. Museums and monuments generally stay open but on reduced timetables, so a packed sightseeing schedule will not survive contact with reality.

**After sunset** everything changes. *Iftar* empties the streets for half an hour and then fills them: markets reopen, cafés overflow, pastry shops do their best trade of the year, families walk late, and mosques fill for *tarawih* prayers. Cities like Algiers and Constantine stay busy well past midnight. This is the part visitors remember.

As a **non-Muslim visitor you are not expected to fast**. What is expected is discretion — not eating, drinking or smoking openly on the street during daylight. Hotels normally continue to serve guests, often discreetly. Nobody will be hostile about it; the courtesy is simply noticed and appreciated.

Two practical notes: book with an operator who reschedules the day around the new rhythm rather than fighting it, and expect the *Eid al-Fitr* holiday at the end to close things for several days. Our [Ramadan in Algeria guide](/blog/algeria-holidays-ramadan/) covers what changes week by week.
