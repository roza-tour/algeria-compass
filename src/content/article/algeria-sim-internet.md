Staying connected in Algeria is straightforward in the north and genuinely impossible in the deep south — and knowing which is which saves a lot of frustration.

## Get a local SIM
Three operators cover the country: **Djezzy**, **Mobilis** and **Ooredoo**. All sell prepaid SIM cards with data bundles that are cheap by European standards, and all are far better value than international roaming.

You will need your **passport** to register the line — this is a legal requirement, not a formality. You can buy at the airport on arrival or at an operator shop in any city.

## What coverage is actually like
- **Northern cities** (Algiers, Oran, Constantine, Annaba, Sétif): good 4G, reliable.
- **Main roads and towns**: generally fine.
- **Mountains** (Kabylie, Aurès, Chréa): patchy — expect dead spots.
- **The deep Sahara** (Tassili, Tadrart, most of the Hoggar): effectively **no signal at all**.

## The Sahara: plan to be offline
This is the part people underestimate. On a desert expedition you will be **uncontactable for days**. Guided trips carry **satellite communication** for emergencies, but that is not for casual use.

Before heading south:
- Download **offline maps** and any documents you need.
- Tell family and colleagues you will be off-grid.
- Don't schedule calls or deadlines during the desert leg.

See our [Sahara travel guide](/blog/algerian-sahara-travel-guide/) for how those trips are structured.

## Wi-Fi and working remotely
Hotel and café Wi-Fi is common in cities but often **slow**; mobile data is usually faster. Algiers and the big cities are workable for light remote work, but Algeria is not a reliable base for video-call-heavy work, and the south is out of the question.

## eSIM and roaming
**eSIM support is limited** and depends on your provider and handset — check before you travel rather than counting on it. **Roaming** works with most international carriers in the north but is expensive; a local SIM is almost always the better call.
