Algeria runs on two calendars at once, and knowing which one your trip lands on changes the experience. National holidays fall on **fixed Gregorian dates**; religious holidays follow the **Islamic lunar calendar**, which moves roughly **11 days earlier each year** and is confirmed by moon sighting. That means the dates below are patterns, not a fixed timetable — always check the current year before booking.

## The fixed national holidays

| Date | Holiday |
|---|---|
| 1 January | New Year's Day |
| **12 January** | **Yennayer** — the Amazigh (Berber) New Year |
| 1 May | Labour Day |
| **5 July** | **Independence Day** (from France, 1962) |
| **1 November** | **Revolution Day** (start of the war of independence, 1954) |

**Yennayer** is worth knowing about: it celebrates the Amazigh new year and is a national holiday, marked with traditional meals and family gatherings — strongest in **Kabylie**, the **Aurès** and the **M'Zab**. See our [culture page](/culture/) for the wider Amazigh context.

## The Islamic calendar

These shift each year:

- **Ramadan** — the month of fasting from dawn to sunset.
- **Eid al-Fitr** (Aïd el-Fitr) — the celebration ending Ramadan; one of the two biggest holidays.
- **Eid al-Adha** (Aïd el-Adha) — the feast of sacrifice; the other major holiday.
- **Awal Muharram** — the Islamic New Year.
- **Ashura** — a day of fasting and remembrance.
- **Mawlid an-Nabawi** (El Mawlid) — the Prophet's birthday.

## Travelling during Ramadan

Ramadan is, honestly, one of the **most rewarding times to see Algeria** — and one that needs a little preparation.

**What changes:**
- Many cafés and restaurants **close during daylight**; hotels usually still serve guests.
- **Opening hours shift** — offices and some sites work shorter mornings.
- Cities become **quiet in the late afternoon**, then come alive after sunset.
- **Iftar** (the breaking of the fast) is a warm, social occasion, and hospitality peaks.

**How to travel respectfully:**
- You are **not expected to fast**, but eat and drink **discreetly** in public during daylight.
- Dress modestly — see our [packing list](/blog/algeria-packing-list/).
- If you're invited to an iftar, accept: it's one of the finest experiences the country offers.

Tours and sites generally keep running, so a Ramadan trip is entirely workable — it simply follows a different rhythm.

## The two Eids

**Eid al-Fitr** and **Eid al-Adha** are the country's biggest family holidays. Expect:
- **Shops and offices closed** for one to two days.
- **Special morning prayers** and large family gatherings.
- **Heavy domestic travel** — transport and hotels fill up.

If your itinerary is tight, plan **around** the Eid days rather than through them, and book transport early. Our [transport guide](/blog/algeria-transportation-guide/) covers the logistics.

## Christmas, Easter and Christian observance

Algeria is a **majority-Muslim country**, and **Christmas and Easter are not public holidays** — 25 December is a normal working day, and you should not expect closures or festive decoration outside a few hotels catering to international guests.

There is, however, a **small Christian community**, and churches hold services. The most visible are in **Algiers** — the **Sacré-Cœur Cathedral** and the hilltop basilica of **Notre-Dame d'Afrique** above the bay — with further churches in **Oran** and **Constantine**. Notre-Dame d'Afrique is a mainstream visitor site year-round and features on our [Algiers day tour](/tours/algierstour-hammagarden-notredame-dafrique/).

Visitors of any faith are treated with courtesy; Algeria's religious heritage — Islamic, Christian and Jewish — is layered and old, which is part of what makes it interesting.

## The working week

The Algerian weekend is **Friday and Saturday**. **Friday** is the main day of congregational prayer, so mornings are quiet and many businesses close. Plan administrative tasks and business meetings for **Sunday to Thursday**.
